# Do Data Centers Raise Electricity Rates? What the Evidence Shows

**Meta description:** Data centers are reshaping America's power grid — and the bill is increasingly landing on households. Here's what the evidence actually shows about data centers and electricity rates, including the industry's counterarguments.

**Key takeaways**
- U.S. residential electricity prices rose roughly 7% in 2025 — more than double the rate of inflation — and analysts project no relief through the end of the decade, with data centers driving roughly 40% of electricity demand growth.
- In the PJM grid region (13 states from Illinois to North Carolina), data centers were responsible for an estimated $9.3 billion increase in capacity-market costs, translating to roughly $16–18/month on typical residential bills in Ohio and western Maryland.
- Peer-reviewed modeling projects average wholesale electricity costs rising 6–29% nationally by 2030; a Carnegie Mellon estimate puts the average household bill increase at 8% by 2030, exceeding 25% in the highest-demand markets like Northern Virginia.
- The industry counterargument — that large, steady loads can *lower* per-unit costs — has real economic logic and some supporting research. The honest conclusion: **impacts depend on who is required to pay for new infrastructure.** That's a policy choice your utility commission makes, and it's where residents have leverage.

---

## The basic mechanics

A single hyperscale data center can draw 100 megawatts or more — roughly the electricity demand of 100,000 homes, per the International Energy Agency. The largest AI campuses now under development are planned in the *gigawatts*: Meta's Hyperion project in Louisiana is designed for at least 5 GW.

Serving loads like that requires new generation, new transmission lines, and new substations. Utilities recover those costs through rates. The central question for every community is simple: **does the data center pay the full cost of the infrastructure built to serve it, or is that cost spread across all ratepayers?**

The scale of what's coming is hard to overstate. Utilities received interconnection requests totaling at least 700 gigawatts of new data center load in 2025 — more than the roughly 477 GW the entire United States consumed in 2023. Even if only a fraction is built, the grid investment required is enormous.

## What's happening to bills right now

- Residential electricity prices jumped about 7% in 2025 year over year — more than double headline inflation — and Goldman Sachs analysts told clients prices will keep rising through the end of the decade as data centers account for roughly 40% of demand growth.
- Utilities requested a record ~$31 billion in rate increases in 2025; requests in the first half of the year alone were double the same period in 2024.
- In PJM's 2025–26 capacity auction, data center demand drove an estimated $9.3 billion in added costs — flowing through to residential bills as roughly +$18/month in western Maryland and +$16/month in Ohio.
- Looking forward, a 2026 study in *Environmental Research Letters* (NC State) modeled national average wholesale costs rising between 6% and 29% by 2030 depending on buildout scenarios; Carnegie Mellon researchers estimate an 8% average U.S. bill increase by 2030, potentially exceeding 25% in central and northern Virginia.

Electricity affordability has already become an election issue: the 2025 governor's races in Virginia and New Jersey were won partly on promises to bring utility bills under control.

## The strongest case on the other side — taken seriously

Per our editorial principles, here is the best version of the industry's argument:

1. **Steady load spreads fixed costs.** Data centers draw power continuously rather than in spiky bursts. Historically, demand growth of this kind has pushed per-unit prices *down* by spreading fixed grid costs over more kilowatt-hours. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory research examining 2019–2024 state data found that states with higher demand growth generally saw *smaller* retail price increases.
2. **State-level price comparisons are ambiguous.** An Institute for Energy Research analysis found the ten states with the most data centers averaged 14.46¢/kWh in 2025 versus 14.39¢ elsewhere — essentially no difference.
3. **Large customers can subsidize the grid.** A 2025 Energy and Environmental Economics analysis found large-load customers often pay more than the minimum cost to serve them; PG&E has projected incremental data center growth could *reduce* average household bills by up to 2%.

## So who's right?

Both things can be true, because the outcome is determined by **rate design, not physics.** Where regulators require data centers to sign long-term contracts covering the full cost of new generation and transmission — with protections if the facility downsizes or leaves — other ratepayers can be insulated or even benefit. Where utilities socialize those costs across all customers, households pay for infrastructure built for a trillion-dollar industry. The PJM capacity-market numbers show what happens in the second scenario.

**What this means for your community:** the highest-leverage questions to ask at any hearing are:
- Will the developer sign a special rate tariff or large-load contract covering 100% of incremental infrastructure costs?
- Who bears the cost if the project is cancelled or scaled back after the utility builds for it?
- Has your state utility commission adopted ratepayer-protection rules for large loads? (Several states are actively considering them — Wisconsin's AB 840, for example, would require the Public Service Commission to shield ratepayers from data center infrastructure costs.)

A data center that pays its own way is a very different proposition from one that doesn't. Make your officials say, on the record, which one is being proposed.

## FAQ

**Will a data center raise my electric bill?**
It depends on how your state assigns infrastructure costs. In the PJM region, data center demand has already added an estimated $9.3 billion to capacity costs, raising typical bills $16–18/month in some states. Where regulators require data centers to fund their own infrastructure through special tariffs, household impacts can be minimal.

**How much electricity does a data center use?**
A typical hyperscale facility uses around 100 MW — comparable to 100,000 homes — and the largest AI campuses are planned at 1–5 gigawatts.

**Are data centers the only reason electricity prices are rising?**
No. Inflation, fuel costs, and aging grid infrastructure also contribute. But data centers are the dominant source of *new* demand — roughly 40% of growth through 2030 per Goldman Sachs — and that demand is driving record utility rate-increase requests.

**What can my community actually do?**
Push for large-load tariffs, full cost-of-service contracts, exit-fee protections, and state ratepayer-protection legislation before approval — not after.
