# How to Fight a Data Center Proposal: A Step-by-Step Playbook

**Meta description:** Communities blocked or stalled at least 48 data center projects worth $156 billion in 2025 alone. This is the playbook — from your first rezoning notice to the final vote — built from what's actually worked.

**Key takeaways**
- This fight is winnable. At least **48 projects representing $156 billion were blocked or stalled by local opposition in 2025**; cancellations jumped from 6 in 2024 to 25 in 2025, with 20+ more killed in Q1 2026 — a record pace. In one tracked quarter, **66% of protested projects were blocked or delayed.**
- Wins come from **land-use law, speed, and turnout** — not outrage. Zoning boards rule on criteria, not feelings; the side that masters the criteria usually prevails.
- Opposition is bipartisan everywhere it appears: Republicans tend to lead on subsidies and grid strain, Democrats on water and environment. Build the coalition accordingly.
- Not every fight ends in "no." Communities have also won enforceable concessions — Lancaster, PA's community benefits agreement included a water-use cap, noise and air controls, and $20 million in community contributions. Know which outcome you're driving toward.

---

## Step 1: Move fast — the clock started before you heard about it

Most projects are months into quiet land assembly, NDAs, and utility negotiations before the first public notice. The moment you hear a rumor:

- Pull the **rezoning or special-use application** from your planning department (it's public).
- Find the **hearing schedule** — planning commission first, then the elected body. Note comment deadlines.
- Identify what approval is actually required. If the land is already zoned for it ("by-right"), your leverage shifts to permits, utilities, and politics — learn this on day one, not at the podium.
- File **public records requests** immediately: NDAs, utility will-serve letters, water capacity studies, correspondence with the developer. (One Wisconsin community learned what was coming because a single resident filed a records request.)

## Step 2: Build the group before the first hearing

- Start with one channel (a Facebook group or mailing list named for your fight), one weekly meeting, and three roles: a **records person**, a **spokesperson**, and a **turnout organizer**.
- Register on NADC's directory so neighbors searching the project find you — and so groups in other towns fighting the same developer can share documents and tactics. Fragmentation is the opposition's biggest historical weakness; 188+ local groups now exist nationwide, and the ones that coordinate win more.
- Recruit deliberately across party lines. This issue polls badly for developers with *everyone*: frame for conservatives (corporate subsidies, property rights, grid costs) and progressives (water, environment, ratepayers) simultaneously.

## Step 3: Demand transparency — secrecy is their weakness

Ask, on the record: *Who is the end user? Has anyone signed an NDA? What are the projected power and water demands?* If officials can't answer, that's your story — "Project Blue"-style secrecy has become a liability that flips votes and makes news. (See our full guide: *What Is a Data Center NDA?*)

## Step 4: Fight on the criteria, not the vibes

Zoning bodies must justify decisions against written standards: comprehensive plan consistency, compatibility with surrounding uses, adequacy of infrastructure, traffic, noise. Your testimony should map point-by-point to those standards.

The issues that appear in virtually every successful campaign — and should anchor yours:
1. **Water** (raised in over 40% of contested projects): demand peak-day numbers, source, drought provisions.
2. **Power and ratepayer impact**: who funds the substations and lines; cite your state's pending ratepayer-protection measures.
3. **Noise**: 24/7 cooling equipment and generator testing; demand nighttime decibel limits at the property line, setbacks, berms.
4. **Incentives**: total abatement value per permanent job (see our jobs guide — Virginia's auditors found these deals don't pay for themselves).
5. **The comprehensive plan**: if the site is designated residential/agricultural, that's often the single strongest legal ground.

Bring one or two credible experts (a hydrologist, an appraiser, a land-use attorney — even a few hours of retained time) rather than twenty angry speakers making the same point. Then *also* bring the twenty speakers, each assigned a different criterion.

## Step 5: Make it loud and legible

- Yard signs and door hangers turn private worry into visible consensus — officials count signs on their commute.
- Pitch local press with documents, not adjectives: the records you FOIA'd, the incentive math, the secrecy. Reporters cover paper.
- Pack every hearing. Boards behave differently in front of 300 residents than 12.

## Step 6: Know your endgames

**Denial.** Projects get withdrawn under pressure as often as voted down — developers pulled a $400M Virginia project the week before its hearing.
**Moratorium.** Communities in at least 14 states have enacted temporary pauses to write proper ordinances first; Apex, NC moved toward a one-year moratorium after a withdrawal. A moratorium converts a sprint you might lose into a rulemaking you can shape.
**A real ordinance.** Setbacks, noise limits, water disclosure, NDA restrictions (Pima County model) — passed *before* the next proposal arrives. The best time to fight a data center is before it's proposed.
**Enforceable conditions or a CBA.** If approval is likely, drive toward binding commitments: Lancaster, PA's agreement capped water use, controlled noise and emissions, and secured $20 million for the community. Aspirational promises in a press release are worth nothing; conditions in a development agreement are enforceable.
**The ballot box.** Accountability is the long game — Warrenton, VA voters replaced their entire town council with project opponents; Cascade Locks, OR voters recalled port officials and the project died. Officials watching those races now vote differently everywhere.

## Step 7: Win or lose, document everything

File your documents, testimony, expert reports, and outcome with NADC's Wins & Losses library. The movement's growing advantage is shared knowledge — every fight that documents itself makes the next town's fight shorter.

## FAQ

**Can a small community really stop a billion-dollar data center?**
Yes — it happened at least 48 times in 2025, affecting $156 billion in investment, and the pace accelerated into 2026. Roughly two-thirds of actively protested projects in one tracked quarter were blocked or delayed.

**What's the single most effective argument?**
Whichever written zoning criterion the project most clearly fails — usually comprehensive-plan inconsistency, water adequacy, or incompatibility with adjacent residential use. Boards need legal grounds to say no; hand them the grounds.

**What if the land is already zoned industrial?**
Leverage shifts to permits (noise, stormwater, air for generators), utility proceedings, development agreements, moratoria, and politics. By-right projects are harder but not untouchable — and the next parcel isn't zoned yet, so pass the ordinance now.

**Is opposing a data center anti-technology?**
No. Demanding transparency, enforceable limits, and a real cost-benefit analysis is how every other industrial land use is governed. Many campaigns end not in denial but in a project the community can actually live with — that's the point.
