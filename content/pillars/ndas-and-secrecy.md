# What Is a Data Center NDA — and Why Did Your County Sign One?

**Meta description:** Across the country, local officials are signing nondisclosure agreements that keep data center proposals secret from the residents they represent. Here's how the practice works, why it's collapsing under scrutiny, and the reforms that are already winning.

**Key takeaways**
- It is now routine for data center developers to require local governments to sign **nondisclosure agreements** before negotiations — meaning your elected officials may legally be barred from telling you who is building next door, how much power and water it will use, or that a project exists at all.
- The pattern is national and documented: investigations found NDAs in at least five Wisconsin communities; in Tucson, a proposed Amazon facility was known only as "Project Blue," and even county supervisors say they voted without knowing the end user's identity or the project's utility impacts.
- The wall is cracking. **Microsoft announced in March 2026 it would stop signing NDAs for data center proposals** and work to terminate existing ones. A U.S. Senate subcommittee is investigating the practice, and NDA-ban bills are moving in multiple states.
- Concrete reforms already exist: Pima County, Arizona now bars NDAs from shielding a project's power and water usage and limits their duration. That's a template any county can adopt — **before** the next "Project" codename arrives.

---

## How the secrecy works

The playbook is consistent across states. A site selector or shell LLC approaches a locality on behalf of an unnamed "Fortune 100 company." Before sharing details, the company requires officials — sometimes the county attorney, sometimes the whole economic development office — to sign an NDA. Land gets optioned through intermediaries. Incentive packages get negotiated. By the time the public learns anything, the deal is largely structured and officials describe it as too far along to change.

Investigative reporting has documented the pattern in detail. In Wisconsin, at least five communities — Beaver Dam, Kenosha, Janesville, Menomonie, and the town of Beloit — signed NDAs covering proposed data centers; in Beloit's case, discussions ran more than a year before any public announcement. Even where no NDA existed, officials in DeForest worked behind the scenes for months before announcing a proposed $12 billion project residents are now fighting. In Mason County, Kentucky, a farmer was offered $10 million for his land by men who declined to name the buyer, the industry, or even themselves. And in Tucson, Arizona, residents and elected officials didn't learn "Project Blue" was Amazon until a document was mistakenly sent to a journalist — a month *after* the county vote. County supervisors there say they approved the project without information about the end user, environmental impact, or utility demands. (The Tucson City Council subsequently blocked it, and the county reformed its NDA process.)

## Why officials sign — the strongest case for the practice

In fairness, the rationale offered by economic development professionals:

1. **Competitive site selection is real.** Companies argue rivals shouldn't learn where they're investing, at what pace; secrecy is standard in early-stage corporate site searches across industries.
2. **Officials feel trapped.** Local leaders describe NDAs as the price of staying in the running — sign, or the project (and its tax base) goes to the next county. As one Kentucky county attorney put it, it's the only way for elected officials to stay informed of the company's plans at all.
3. **Early-stage proposals are speculative.** Many scouted sites never advance; officials argue publicizing every inquiry would cause needless alarm.

The rebuttal is straightforward, and increasingly bipartisan: there is a difference between a company keeping *its own* plans quiet and a company contractually silencing *the public's representatives* about the use of public water, public power infrastructure, public roads, and public tax dollars. Transparency advocates also note the practice undermines the public's own negotiating position — open competition among bidders tends to produce better deals for taxpayers, which is precisely why developers prefer it closed. And the political spectrum of critics is wide: the NDA-ban bill in Wisconsin came from a Republican representative; the Pennsylvania bill from a Democrat; a Republican state senator there asked simply why the ultimate owner of any major development should ever be hidden from the public.

## The dam is breaking

- **Microsoft**, in March 2026, announced it would stop signing NDAs for data center development and work with local governments to terminate existing ones — framing it as rebuilding community trust. Watchdogs noted the obvious: companies don't make that announcement unless communities are already pushing back hard. The pledge came after Microsoft proposals advanced without NDAs in Mount Pleasant and were withdrawn in Caledonia, Wisconsin.
- **Congress is probing.** Sen. Richard Blumenthal, ranking member of the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, has sought documents on how data center operators use NDAs to limit what state and local officials can share.
- **States are legislating.** Wisconsin's Senate Bill 969 (prohibiting local-government data center NDAs) advanced from committee; Pennsylvania's Rep. Joe Ciresi filed a state NDA ban; chambers in New York and Michigan have passed restrictions.
- **Counties aren't waiting.** Pima County, Arizona's post-"Project Blue" reforms require disclosure of project details within 90 days of a public vote and prohibit NDA provisions that conceal projected power and water usage.

## What to do in your community

1. **Ask the question on the record.** At any public meeting: "Has the county or any official signed a nondisclosure agreement related to a data center or unnamed industrial project? Please answer yes or no." Officials can typically acknowledge an NDA's *existence* even when bound by its contents — and a refusal to answer is itself an answer.
2. **File public records requests** for NDAs themselves, utility will-serve letters, water/power capacity inquiries, and correspondence with site selectors. (A records request by a Port Washington, Wisconsin resident is how that community learned what was coming.)
3. **Push a local NDA ordinance** modeled on Pima County: duration limits, mandatory disclosure windows before any vote, and no concealment of power/water demands.
4. **Make it electoral.** Officials who sign secrecy deals should expect the question at every forum. As the sponsor of Wisconsin's ban put it — voters should remember who signed.

## FAQ

**Are data center NDAs legal?**
Generally yes, today — which is why reform efforts focus on banning or limiting them by statute and ordinance. Several states are advancing exactly that.

**Can my official tell me anything once they've signed?**
Usually they can confirm an NDA exists and describe the process, even if project specifics are restricted. They can also decline to sign in the first place — Microsoft built one of its largest campuses, in Mount Pleasant, WI, without one.

**Why do companies say NDAs are necessary?**
Competitive secrecy during site selection. The counterargument: protecting corporate strategy never requires silencing public officials about public resource commitments — and at least one major hyperscaler has now abandoned the practice.

**What's the best model reform?**
Pima County, Arizona: NDAs cannot conceal projected power and water usage, are time-limited, and full disclosure is required well before any public vote.
