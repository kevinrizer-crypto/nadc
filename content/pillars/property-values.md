# Do Data Centers Affect Property Values? An Honest Look at Thin Evidence

**Meta description:** The research on data centers and home values is newer and more mixed than either side admits. Here's what the studies actually found, why they may not apply to your town, and the questions that matter more than averages.

**Key takeaways**
- The most-cited formal study (George Mason University, Northern Virginia) found **no statistical evidence that proximity to a data center reduces home values** — in fact, homes nearer data centers sold for more. Developers now cite it at hearings nationwide.
- A separate Indiana analysis found values near data centers **rose, but more slowly than the surrounding county in 3 of 4 cases** studied.
- Both findings come with serious limitations: small samples, unique markets, and facilities that were sited before the current wave of massive AI campuses next to subdivisions.
- The credible concern isn't regional price collapse — it's **adjacency**: noise, vibration, lighting, truck traffic, and new transmission corridors affecting the specific homes closest to a facility. Averages don't capture the family whose backyard now borders forty acres of cooling fans.

**We publish the studies that cut against our position. That's the point of this site.** A movement that only cites favorable evidence gets dismissed; one that engages all of it gets taken seriously.

## What the studies actually say

**The George Mason study (Northern Virginia).** Researchers at GMU's Center for Regional Analysis examined 2023 home sales against locations of data centers built or permitted by August 2024 in Northern Virginia — the densest data center market on Earth. Across 13 tested factors, they predicted greater distance from a data center would add value. The data said otherwise: homes closer to data centers sold for *more* on average, and the study concluded it could not find statistical evidence that proximity reduces housing values. The researchers' own explanation: data centers cluster where infrastructure, jobs, and amenities already make neighborhoods desirable — meaning the correlation likely reflects *where data centers get built*, not what they do to prices.

**The Indiana analysis.** Integra Realty Resources examined estimated home values (2021–present) within 1.5 miles of four Indiana data centers (Microsoft LaPorte, Amazon New Carlisle, Google Fort Wayne, Meta Jeffersonville). Values rose near all four — but in three of four cases, they rose *less* than the rest of the county (e.g., LaPorte: +42% nearby vs. +48% countywide). Notably, this analysis was presented by a developer's side in support of a zoning variance.

**That's essentially the whole formal literature.** As industry publication Data Center Knowledge itself notes, no study yet evaluates impacts across a wide range of regions and facility types. Anyone — on either side — claiming the question is settled is overstating the evidence.

## Why the Northern Virginia result may not travel

1. **Selection, not causation.** The GMU authors attribute their finding to data centers locating near good infrastructure and jobs. A facility dropped onto farmland beside a rural subdivision — the typical new AI campus — shares none of those confounders.
2. **Vintage matters.** NoVA's stock includes many older, smaller facilities. Today's proposals are 10–50x larger, with on-site generation, gigawatt substations, and miles of new transmission line. The Indiana analysis, covering newer hyperscale sites, found the relative underperformance.
3. **Averages hide adjacency.** Regional sales data can't capture the household experiencing 24/7 cooling-fan hum. Reporting from Virginia documents residents describing constant industrial noise and vibration inside their homes — in one Prince William County case, a family moving their newborn to the basement because of it — and transmission corridors crossing private property. These costs are real, concentrated, and invisible in market-wide regressions.

## What to argue at a hearing (and what not to)

**Don't** claim data centers will crater home values town-wide. The evidence doesn't support it, the developer's attorney will cite GMU, and you'll lose credibility you need for stronger arguments.

**Do** focus on what the evidence and common sense support:
- **Adjacency impacts are unstudied and uncompensated.** Demand setbacks (many model ordinances now use 500+ feet from residential property lines), berms, sound walls, and enforceable decibel limits at the property line — measured at night, including backup generator testing.
- **Disclosure of the full footprint.** Substations, transmission routes, and on-site generation often appear *after* approval. Require the complete infrastructure plan up front.
- **Expansion rights.** A facility 0.5 miles away today may be 0.25 miles away after the next phase. Demand the full buildout plan and condition approval on it.
- **The uncertainty itself.** When the only national evidence is two limited studies, the burden of proof on "no harm" belongs to the party making billions, not the homeowners absorbing the risk. An independent, locality-specific impact study is a reasonable condition of approval.

## FAQ

**Will a data center lower my home's value?**
The honest answer: the limited research doesn't show town-wide declines, and one major study found higher prices near data centers in Northern Virginia. But evidence is thin, newer mega-campuses are largely unstudied, and homes immediately adjacent face real noise, lighting, traffic, and transmission-line impacts that averages don't capture.

**What's the best evidence that data centers don't hurt home values?**
The 2025 George Mason University analysis of Northern Virginia sales — read it, because the developer's team will. Its authors attribute the result to data centers being built in already-desirable, infrastructure-rich areas.

**What's the best evidence they might?**
The Indiana Integra analysis found nearby values grew more slowly than county-wide values in 3 of 4 cases, and documented adjacency complaints (noise, vibration, transmission corridors) are widespread in mature markets.

**What protections should neighbors demand?**
Setbacks from residential lines, nighttime decibel limits measured at the property line, generator-testing schedules, full disclosure of substations and transmission routes, and conditions binding future expansion.
