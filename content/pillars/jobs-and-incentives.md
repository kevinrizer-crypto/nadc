# How Many Jobs Does a Data Center Actually Create?

**Meta description:** Data centers generate real construction jobs and real tax revenue — and remarkably few permanent jobs for the land, power, and subsidies they consume. Here's what Virginia's own state auditors found, and the questions to ask before your town trades incentives for promises.

**Key takeaways**
- The most authoritative source — Virginia's nonpartisan legislative audit agency (JLARC), studying the largest data center market in the world — found data centers deliver economic benefits **mostly during initial construction**, and employ comparatively few people long-term.
- The scale of subsidies is staggering: data centers were the **largest beneficiary of Virginia's economic development incentive spending over the last decade — 53% of it, at least $2.7 billion** — despite supporting few permanent jobs. JLARC concluded the exemption "does not pay for itself," returning about 48 cents in state revenue per dollar exempted.
- Virginia's incentive threshold tells you the industry's own math: qualifying requires $150 million in capital investment but only **50 jobs** (25 in distressed areas). That's $3 million of investment per promised job.
- The construction boom is real — tens of thousands of temporary jobs and billions in activity — and so is local property tax revenue. The honest framing isn't "no jobs"; it's **jobs per acre, per megawatt, and per subsidy dollar**, where data centers rank near the bottom of all development types.

---

## What Virginia's own auditors found

No one has studied this question with more rigor or less motive than Virginia's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission — the state legislature's nonpartisan watchdog, examining the industry in its global capital.

JLARC's 2024 report found data centers provide positive economic benefits to Virginia's economy **mostly because of construction-phase capital investment**, and that the facilities employ fewer people than other industries once operating. Between 2021 and 2023, the industry was associated with 50,700 jobs and $7.2 billion in economic contribution in Northern Virginia — but those figures include temporary construction and indirect employment; the bulk of supported jobs are temporary roles.

On the subsidy side, JLARC's December 2025 follow-up found data centers were by far the largest beneficiary of state incentive spending over the past decade — 53% of all of it, at minimum $2.7 billion in sales tax exemptions — figures the agency notes are likely *under*estimates due to record-keeping changes. The 2024 report classified the exemption as a "moderate economic benefit" and stated plainly that, like most development incentives, it does not pay for itself: roughly 48 cents of new state revenue per dollar exempted.

## Why permanent job counts are so low

A modern hyperscale facility is, operationally, a highly automated warehouse for servers. Once built, it needs security, facilities technicians, and a small operations team. Virginia's own incentive design reflects this: the sales-tax exemption requires just 50 new jobs alongside $150 million in capital investment. Compare that with what 100+ acres of land, a nine-figure incentive package, and 100+ megawatts of regional power capacity would yield in almost any other use — a hospital, a manufacturing plant, a mixed-use district — and the opportunity cost becomes the real story.

When a developer's economic impact study projects thousands of jobs, ask for the breakdown: **construction vs. permanent, direct vs. "induced," and on-site vs. regional.** Induced-jobs multipliers do most of the inflating.

## The strongest case on the other side — taken seriously

1. **Construction jobs are genuinely large.** Multi-year, multi-billion-dollar builds employ thousands of tradespeople at good wages. In regions with strong building-trades unions, this is a real, bankable benefit — just a temporary one.
2. **Local property tax revenue can be transformative.** Loudoun County, Virginia funds a major share of its budget from data center taxes. Where facilities pay full freight on real and personal property taxes, the fiscal benefit per resident-service-demanded is excellent — data centers don't send kids to school or call 911 often.
3. **Compared to other incentives, the return isn't the worst.** JLARC found Virginia's data center exemption returned 48 cents per dollar versus an average of 17 cents for other sales-tax incentive programs — partly because it's one of the few with a job requirement at all.
4. **The investment is mobile.** JLARC-cited analysis found ~90% of incentivized investment would have gone to other states without the exemption. The industry's leverage in a state-vs-state bidding war is real.

The rebuttal to #2 and #4 is the same: **the fiscal case depends entirely on the deal your locality actually signs.** Loudoun's revenue comes from taxing data centers heavily. Many localities instead grant decades-long property tax abatements, PILOT agreements, and exemptions that give away precisely the benefit that justified the project. And the state-vs-state race to the bottom is an argument for *collective* reform, not for your town unilaterally surrendering.

## Questions that cut through the jobs pitch

1. How many **permanent, on-site, W-2 jobs** — not construction, not induced — and at what wages? Will the developer accept clawbacks if targets aren't met?
2. What is the **total incentive package** — state exemptions plus local abatements plus infrastructure paid by the public — divided by those permanent jobs?
3. What **property tax revenue** will the facility actually pay in years 1, 5, 10, and 20 under the proposed agreement — not at standard rates it's been exempted from?
4. What does the same land, power, and water yield in **alternative development**?
5. Will hiring commitments be **local**, and are they enforceable or aspirational?

## FAQ

**How many permanent jobs does a typical data center create?**
Far fewer than most industrial projects of comparable investment. Virginia's incentive program requires only 50 jobs per $150 million invested, and the state's legislative auditors found the industry employs comparatively few people long-term, with benefits concentrated in temporary construction.

**Don't data centers pay a lot of taxes?**
They can — where localities tax them fully, as Loudoun County, VA does. But many deals include long-term abatements and exemptions that surrender much of that revenue; Virginia alone has foregone at least $2.7 billion over a decade. The tax benefit is a function of the deal, not the building.

**Do data center incentives pay for themselves?**
Virginia's JLARC says no — about 48 cents returned per dollar exempted, though better than the state's average incentive program.

**What should my town demand?**
Enforceable job and wage commitments with clawbacks, full disclosure of all incentives, property taxation at or near standard rates, and an independent (not developer-funded) fiscal impact analysis.
